Hazard perception explained

Hazard perception is the second part of the theory test. This page explains how GOV.UK describes it, then gives learning ideas drawn from the Highway Code. It does not include practice clips: for official video practice, GOV.UK points to the DVSA’s own products.

How it works (from GOV.UK)

  • Before you start, you are shown a video about how the test works. Then you watch 14 video clips of everyday road scenes.
  • Each clip has at least one ‘developing hazard’, and one clip has two.
  • A developing hazard is something that would cause you to take action, like changing speed or direction.
  • You score up to 5 points per developing hazard, for clicking as soon as it starts to develop. You do not lose points for a wrong click, but you score nothing if you click continuously or in a pattern.
  • You get one attempt at each clip and cannot review or change your responses.
  • The pass mark is 44 out of 75. You must pass this and the multiple-choice part to pass the theory test.

Source: GOV.UK, Theory test: hazard perception test, read on 24 November 2026.

Source: GOV.UK, Theory test: pass mark and test result, read on 24 November 2026.

GOV.UK’s example

A car is parked at the side of the road and is not doing anything. It would not cause you to take action, so it is not a developing hazard. As you get closer, its right-hand indicator starts to flash and it begins to move away. You would need to slow down, so it is now a developing hazard.

The lesson: a potential hazard becomes a developing hazard when it changes in a way that would make you act. Scan for things that might change, and watch them.

What to look out for

These are this site’s own pointers, each tied to Highway Code rules. They are a way of thinking, not a list of what appears in the test.

Parked cars and gaps between them
Children, doors opening and drivers pulling out are all possible. Take care passing parked vehicles, especially where children may be near. Rule 206
Cyclists, motorcyclists and horse riders
They are hard to see at junctions and roundabouts, and may need to move around drains or wet patches. A rider looking over a shoulder may be about to pull out. Rule 211, Rule 212, Rule 213
Pedestrians near schools, shops and bus stops
People may step out suddenly, children especially. Buses pulling away deserve priority when it is safe. Rule 205, Rule 206, Rule 208, Rule 223
Bends and hidden junctions on country roads
Bends can be sharper than they look, and junctions partly hidden. Slow farm vehicles, riders and mud are all possible. Rule 154
Large vehicles
They need more room to turn and block your view. Hang back to see more. Rule 221, Rule 222
Emergency vehicles and sudden changes ahead
Flashing lights ahead can mean an incident. Slow down and be ready to move. Rule 219, Rule 281
